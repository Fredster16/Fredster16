## Part 1: Book Map

**Title:** *Leaders Eat Last: Why Some Teams Pull Together and Others Don't*
**Author:** Simon Sinek
**Central claim:** People work together, trust each other and perform best when leaders put their people's safety and well-being ahead of numbers and their own interests. This creates a "Circle of Safety" that works with our biology rather than against it.

### Chapters
*(Chapters 7–27 are summarised from a quick skim. Full breakdowns will follow in later batches.)*

**Part 1: Our Need to Feel Safe**
1. **Protection from Above:** Great organizations have leaders who give cover from above and people who protect each other, and empathy makes this possible.
2. **Employees Are People Too:** When leaders treat employees as trusted people, the way a parent treats a child, those employees commit to each other and the organization thrives.
3. **Belonging:** A leader's main job is to build a Circle of Safety that removes internal threats, so people can focus on external dangers and opportunities.
4. **Yeah, but . . .:** Staying in an unsafe workplace costs our health, performance and families, because stress comes from a lack of control rather than from rank.

**Part 2: Powerful Forces**
5. **When Enough Was Enough:** Humans evolved to survive scarcity by cooperating, and four chemicals reward behaviour that helps either the individual or the group.
6. **E.D.S.O.:** Endorphins and dopamine drive individual achievement, while serotonin and oxytocin drive trust, loyalty and cooperation. Each has its benefits and its risks.
7. **The Big C:** Cortisol, the stress chemical, is an early-warning system. In a workplace without a Circle of Safety it stays switched on, damaging health, trust and cooperation.
8. **Why We Have Leaders:** Groups give leaders (alphas) status and perks in return for protecting the group, so leadership is a responsibility to "eat last."

**Part 3: Reality**
9. **The Courage to Do the Right Thing:** People do the right thing, even breaking rules when needed, when leaders trust them and give them control.
10. **Snowmobile in the Desert:** Modern life makes cooperation harder. Trust and commitment cannot be ordered; they take time and the right conditions.

**Part 4: How We Got Here**
11. **The Boom Before the Bust:** Economic booms create imbalances that nature corrects. A prosperous postwar era raised the Baby Boomers to put themselves first.
12. **The Boomers All Grown Up:** Boomer leadership normalized layoffs and leaders "eating first." Abundance and scale turned people into abstractions.

**Part 5: The Abstract Challenge**
13. **Abstraction Kills:** Milgram's obedience experiments show that distance from the people affected makes us more willing to harm them.
14. **Modern Abstraction:** Large companies run from spreadsheets make harmful decisions. A higher moral purpose helps leaders resist this.
15. **Managing the Abstraction:** Five rules for reducing abstraction:
    - keep it real
    - obey Dunbar's number
    - meet the people you help
    - give time, not just money
    - be patient
16. **Imbalance:** "Destructive Abundance" happens when selfish, dopamine-driven pursuits overwhelm selfless ones and results are protected above people.

**Part 6: Destructive Abundance**
17. **Leadership Lesson 1, So Goes the Culture, so Goes the Company:** Whether a culture is sacrificed or protected decides whether a company fails or keeps innovating.
18. **Leadership Lesson 2, So Goes the Leader, so Goes the Culture:** Leaders who give control to people closer to the work make the organization more powerful and produce more leaders.
19. **Leadership Lesson 3, Integrity Matters:** Trust comes from telling the truth. Spin destroys it.
20. **Leadership Lesson 4, Friends Matter:** Personal relationships are a precondition for cooperation, for example between rival political parties.
21. **Leadership Lesson 5, Lead the People, Not the Numbers:** Profit is fuel for a culture, not the main goal, and customers only love a company after its employees do.

**Part 7: A Society of Addicts**
22. **At the Center of All Our Problems Is Us:** Company incentive systems encourage dopamine addiction to performance, at the cost of cooperation.
23. **At Any Expense:** Performance addiction and deregulation have broken the wider societal Circle of Safety.
24. **The Abstract Generation:** A "Distracted Generation" hooked on dopamine from devices and social media is inheriting the "Me Generation's" problems.

**Part 8: Becoming a Leader**
25. **Step 12:** Like AA's twelfth step, service to real people is the cure for dopamine addiction, and human bonds help us endure hardship.
26. **Shared Struggle:** People are inspired by struggling together for a cause bigger than the company, not by selfish goals.
27. **We Need More Leaders:** Anyone can choose to be the leader they wish they had.

### How the Main Ideas Connect
- **Biology underpins everything:** The four "happy" chemicals (E.D.S.O.) and cortisol (Ch. 5–7) explain why the Circle of Safety (Ch. 3) works.
- **Circle of Safety → performance:** Removing internal threats (Ch. 3) frees energy for external threats and opportunities. Trust, cooperation and innovation are results of this, not things that can be ordered.
- **Leadership comes first:** Leaders create the Circle (Ch. 2–3, 8). The perks of status (Ch. 8) are paid in exchange for protecting the group. When leaders "eat first" (Ch. 12), the Circle breaks.
- **Control → stress and health:** A sense of control (Ch. 4) depends on being trusted, which leads to courage and good judgment (Ch. 9).
- **Selfish vs. selfless balance:** Dopamine is needed for progress (Ch. 6). An imbalance in its favour causes Destructive Abundance (Ch. 16–17) and addiction (Ch. 22–24).
- **Abstraction weakens empathy:** Scale and distance (Ch. 12–14) block the empathy and serotonin/oxytocin bonds the Circle needs. The fixes are in Ch. 15.
- **Service is the remedy:** Service to known people (Ch. 25) and shared struggle for a real cause (Ch. 26) rebalance the chemicals and rebuild the Circle.

## Part 2: Chapter Breakdowns

### Chapter 1: Protection from Above (pp. 3–8)
- **In one sentence:** The best organizations have leaders who give cover from above and people who look out for each other, and empathy is how any organization gets there.
- **Ideas:**
  - Knowing someone is watching over them gives people on the front line more confidence, and the protector more assurance that they can help. (p. 4)
  - Empathy, sensing how others feel even when you cannot see them, can move someone to act without being ordered to. (pp. 4–5)
  - Empathy is a learnable discipline: deliberately imagine what the people you protect are going through. (p. 5)
  - In linear hierarchies, people work for recognition from the person above them. This is fragile, because it only works while that one supervisor stays and is under no undue pressure. (p. 7)
  - In a culture of sacrifice and service, the drive to advance the organization comes from protection at every level, not from recognition from above. (p. 7)
  - Empathy, not training, education or tools, is the greatest asset for taking risks on behalf of others. The motive is reciprocity: "they would have done it for me." (p. 7)
  - Such courage is not only inborn. If working conditions meet a certain standard, anyone is capable of it, and colleagues will do the same for us. (pp. 7–8)
  - Bonds like these produce success and fulfillment that money, fame or awards cannot buy. (p. 8)
  - The best organizations, which are the most innovative, respected and loyal and the most resilient in a crisis, share one pattern: leaders provide cover from above and people look out for each other. This is why they are willing to take risks. (p. 8)
  - Any organization can achieve this through empathy. (p. 8)
- **Terms:** none introduced.
- **Example:** An A-10 pilot sensed the anxiety of 22 troops under fire, dropped below the clouds without being ordered to, and protected them. None were killed. (pp. 3–6)
- **Quote:** "And the way any organization can achieve this is with empathy." (p. 8)

### Chapter 2: Employees Are People Too (pp. 9–18)
- **In one sentence:** When leaders extend trust and treat employees as people they are responsible for, the way parents treat children, those employees protect each other and the organization thrives.
- **Ideas:**
  - Start by listening to employees rather than imposing a strategy. (pp. 9–10)
  - Controls that signal distrust, such as time clocks, bells and locked supply cages, tell people management does not trust them, even if the controls prevent theft. (pp. 10–11)
  - Treat everyone the same, whatever their role or education. Unequal trust splits one company into two. (pp. 10–11)
  - To earn trust, leaders must first treat people like people and extend trust first, without asking for anything in return. (p. 11)
  - Changing the environment, not the people, changes how people behave. When people feel cared for, they care for others and give their "heads and hearts." (pp. 11, 15)
  - Trusted people work harder to keep that trust. They also look after the company's assets better, which lowers costs. (p. 12)
  - A caring culture drives growth through existing staff, without bonuses, threats or reorganization. Obligation is replaced by pride, and people come to work for each other. (p. 12)
  - Truly human leadership means creating an environment where people can express themselves honestly and be recognized and celebrated for their progress. (p. 14)
  - When people must manage dangers from inside the organization, it is less able to face dangers from outside. Internal trust and cooperation make it stronger. (p. 14)
  - The body's systems for protecting us respond to our environment:
    - When we sense danger, our defenses go up.
    - When we feel safe among our own group, we relax and become open to trust and cooperation. (p. 14)
  - High-performing organizations resemble the conditions humans evolved for. When people feel safe with each other, they achieve together what none could alone. This is biology and anthropology, not management theory or hiring "dream teams." (pp. 14–15)
  - Our primitive systems still read the modern world as either threats or safety. Understanding these systems helps both individuals and groups succeed. (p. 15)
  - Most organizations' norms work against our biology. As a result, most employees are dissatisfied (80%, per the Deloitte Shift Index), progress costs more and lasts less, and success is measured in quarters, not decades. (p. 15)
  - Short-term, money-before-people business harms society, because people take their unhappiness at work home with them. (pp. 15–16)
  - Coming home from work feeling inspired, safe, fulfilled and grateful is a natural right, not a luxury. (p. 16)
  - Transformation comes from many small, gut-led changes, not one big change. (p. 16)
  - Leadership is like parenthood. Each employee is someone's child, entrusted to the company, and leaders are responsible for their lives and growth. (pp. 16–17)
  - When leaders care for people like family, people become loyal, defend the company and colleagues, and take the company's name as part of their identity. (p. 17)
  - Capitalism works better when people work as they were designed to: inspired to cooperate, trust and stay loyal. Sacrifice the numbers to save the people, not the reverse. (p. 17)
  - Putting people first does not sacrifice performance. These organizations are among the most stable, innovative and high-performing in their industries. (p. 17)
  - Great leaders treat money as the resource that grows people, not people as the resource that grows money. Performance matters because it fuels a stronger organization that serves its people. (pp. 17–18)
  - Lasting success comes from growing people's ability to do what needs to be done. Great people make the leader look like a genius, not the reverse. (p. 18)
  - This is not idealism. Such organizations already exist in every sector. (p. 18)
  - Organizations face enough danger from outside. Adding internal threats has no value. (p. 18)
  - Leaders' responsibility is to protect their people, who will then protect each other and advance the organization. (p. 18)
  - Employees need the courage to care for each other when leaders don't. By doing so, they become the leaders they wish they had. (p. 18)
- **Terms:**
  - **Truly human leadership:** Chapman's term for creating an environment where people can express themselves honestly and be recognized and celebrated for their progress. (p. 14)
- **Example:** At HayssenSandiacre, removing time clocks, bells and locked parts cages led to near-zero theft, fewer machine breakdowns and revenue growth from $55M to $95M. (pp. 10–12)
- **Quote:** "To sacrifice the numbers to save the people and not sacrifice the people to save the numbers." (p. 17)

### Chapter 3: Belonging (pp. 19–25)
- **In one sentence:** A leader's main role is to build and widen a Circle of Safety in which people feel they belong, so their energy goes to outside threats and opportunities rather than to protecting themselves from each other.
- **Ideas:**
  - Turning "me" into "we" creates belonging, shared values and empathy. These greatly improve trust, cooperation and problem-solving. (pp. 19–20)
  - A group that fears no danger from within is better able to face danger from outside. (p. 20)
  - Job skills are not what make high-performing groups perform. What matters is how well people pull together, and that depends on the environment. (p. 20)
  - External dangers (markets, new technology, competitors, outside pressures) are constant, beyond our control and permanent. (p. 21)
  - Internal dangers are variable and within our control. They include:
    - layoffs
    - fear of being punished for failed risks
    - politics
    - intimidation, humiliation, isolation and rejection (pp. 21–22)
  - Leadership's goal is a culture free of danger from each other. How to build it:
    - give people a sense of belonging
    - build a strong culture on clear human values and beliefs
    - give people the power to make decisions
    - offer trust and empathy (p. 22)
  - Without a Circle of Safety, people spend their time and energy protecting themselves from each other. (p. 22)
  - The more we trust that the people beside us have our backs, the better we face outside threats together. (p. 22)
  - An organization's strength comes from how well its people pull together, not from its products. Everyone helps maintain the Circle, and the leader's primary role is to make sure they do. (pp. 22–23)
  - Leaders are gatekeepers. They set the standards for entry, and should judge character and cultural fit, not only grades or résumés. Admitting someone is like adopting a child. (p. 23)
  - Entry standards based on clear human values strengthen belonging and willingness to contribute. (p. 23)
  - Leaders decide how wide the Circle extends:
    - Small organizations are easier to keep safe but more exposed to outside danger.
    - As organizations grow, leaders must rely on layers of management. If those managers protect themselves, progress slows and the organization becomes vulnerable. (p. 23)
  - Weak leaders extend the Circle only to an inner circle. Everyone else is left to protect themselves, which leads to silos, politics, hidden mistakes, slow information and unease. (p. 23)
  - Strong leaders extend the Circle to every person, so self-preservation and fiefdoms are unnecessary. (p. 24)
  - We can feel when we are in the Circle: we feel valued by colleagues, cared for by superiors and sure that others will help us succeed. (p. 24)
  - Inside the Circle, information flows freely. This drives innovation, stops problems escalating and helps the organization defend itself and seize opportunities. (p. 24)
  - Without the Circle, paranoia, cynicism and self-interest take over. (p. 24)
  - Trust, big ideas and cooperation cannot be ordered. They result from feeling safe and trusted. In a strong Circle, people share ideas, information and stress, and every strength is amplified. (p. 24)
  - Leaders need safety too. Members owe their leaders concern, for example by asking "Are you okay?" when a boss is harsh for no clear reason. That keeps the Circle strong. (pp. 24–25)
- **Terms:**
  - **Circle of Safety:** A protective environment, created by leadership, in which people feel they belong and fear no danger from each other, so they can face external dangers together. (pp. 20–22)
- **Example:** Spartans were punished for losing their shield, not their helmet or breastplate, because the shield protected the whole line. (p. 22)
- **Quote:** "This is the primary role of leadership, to look out for those inside their Circle." (p. 23)

### Chapter 4: Yeah, but . . . (pp. 26–32)
- **In one sentence:** Putting up with an unsafe workplace for the sake of "stability" costs our health, our organizations' performance and our children's well-being, because stress comes from a lack of control rather than from rank.
- **Ideas:**
  - Common objection: people feel trapped by bills and family. Leaders say putting people first is expensive, "soft," hard to measure and impossible under Wall Street, board or competitive pressure. (pp. 26–27)
  - Every decision has a cost. The perceived stability of staying put is paid for with joy, and ultimately with health. (p. 27)
  - Job security is largely a myth. Layoffs used to hit annual targets make jobs a matter of arithmetic, not merit, and the risk is renewed every year. (p. 27)
  - A job we hate can be as bad for our health as unemployment, or worse, producing equal or higher depression and anxiety (2011 University of Canberra study). (pp. 27–28)
  - Work stress comes more from weak management and leadership than from the work itself:
    - Knowing someone cares lowers stress.
    - Leaders who care about numbers over people raise it.
    - Without belonging, people feel no loyalty beyond pay. (p. 28)
  - Feeling unrecognized for one's effort is linked to a higher risk of heart disease, through a lack of control and an effort–reward imbalance (2011 University College London study). (p. 28)
  - Recognition drives engagement. Active disengagement is:
    - 40% when ignored by the boss
    - 22% when regularly criticized
    - 1% when the boss recognizes one strength
    - (Gallup 2013) (p. 28)
  - Unhappy workers make the people around them unhappy too. Companies that tolerate misery suffer the most. (p. 28)
  - Stress is caused by how much control people feel they have, not by the demands of rank. Effort is not stressful in itself; an imbalance between effort and reward is. Less control means more stress (Whitehall Studies). (p. 29)
  - Leaders have lower stress (cortisol) than those who work for them. Feeling in charge of one's life outweighs extra responsibility (2012 Harvard/Stanford study). (pp. 29–30)
  - The lower someone's rank, the higher their risk of stress-related illness and death. The lowest grades had four times the early death rate of the top, and low-control jobs are linked to mental illness. (p. 30)
  - We cannot remove hierarchy, as we are evolutionarily programmed for it, and climbing the hierarchy is not the cure. The issue is our sense of control over our work and lives. (p. 30)
  - Conversely, a supportive, well-managed workplace is good for health:
    - People empowered to make decisions suffer less stress.
    - People who only follow orders suffer most.
    - Control, stress and performance all depend on how safe we feel. (p. 30)
  - Work is the modern tribe. Feeling unsafe among our own tribe violates how we are designed to live. (p. 30)
  - Despite the evidence, people stay in jobs they hate, believing unknown outside dangers are worse than known inside ones. A third consider leaving but under 1.5% leave (Mercer). (pp. 30–31)
  - The alternative to quitting is to stay and change how we act: shift focus from ourselves to the people beside us and offer them protection. (p. 31)
  - Treating people well pays in any economy:
    - In bad times it prevents an exodus when conditions improve.
    - In good times it ensures people rally when hard times return.
    - The best companies survive hard times because their people rally. (p. 31)
  - Children's well-being depends less on parents' working hours than on the mood parents bring home from work. Tolerating a miserable job may harm the children we are providing for (Boston College study). (pp. 31–32)
  - The only solution is to build and maintain Circles of Safety, pulling together rather than pointing fingers. Biological forces can help. (p. 32)
- **Terms:**
  - **Executive stress syndrome:** The popular (and mistaken) belief that the pressures of senior rank make executives the most stressed. (p. 29)
  - **Whitehall Studies:** British studies of civil servants showing that stress and health risk come from low control and effort–reward imbalance, not from high rank. (p. 29)
- **Example:** none needed.
- **Quote:** "Put simply: less control, more stress." (p. 29)

### Chapter 5: When Enough Was Enough (pp. 33–38)
- **In one sentence:** Humans evolved to survive scarcity and danger by cooperating, and our bodies use chemicals that reward behaviour serving either the individual or the group.
- **Ideas:**
  - Our species was built for great danger and insufficient resources. Opportunity came from will and hard work. (p. 33)
  - Paleolithic humans were as smart and capable as we are. They lacked only modern advantages. (p. 34)
  - Our physiology and need to cooperate exist for survival, and we are at our best facing danger together. Leaders who manufacture internal urgency or pressure to motivate people go against biology. (p. 34)
  - With belonging and trust, we cooperate against outside threats. Without it, we spend energy protecting ourselves from each other, become more vulnerable and miss outside opportunities. (p. 34)
  - Humans survived thanks to the neocortex (problem-solving and complex language) and, critically, cooperation. We are a social species that depends on others. (p. 34)
  - Cooperation let us shape our environment to suit us rather than adapt to it. Our genetic coding is unchanged, though, so we are old-fashioned animals in a modern, resource-rich world, which has costs. (pp. 34–35)
  - Ancestral communities of up to about 150 people trusted that helping the group served each member. Despite internal conflict, members united against outsiders. "We always protect our own." (p. 35)
  - Betraying the group harms its survival, which is why treason is punished like murder. Cooperation and mutual aid beat competition and rugged individualism. (p. 35)
  - The inclination to cooperate comes from human biology, not environment. It is universal across cultures. (p. 35)
  - Social time outside work builds trust; familiarity strengthens bonds. Shared meals, conferences, picnics and watercooler time matter, as do leaders roaming the halls beyond meetings. (p. 36)
  - Our bodies reward helping each other so that we keep doing it. (p. 36)
  - Every feature of our physiology, including feelings of happiness, evolved to help us survive. (p. 36)
  - Like bosses using rewards and punishments, nature conditions behaviour with positive and negative feelings that promote getting things done and cooperating. We are all "chemical-dependent." (p. 37)
  - Four chemicals produce all our positive ("happy") feelings: endorphins, dopamine, serotonin and oxytocin. Each serves survival, not merely pleasure. (p. 37)
  - Humans are always both individuals and group members. Decisions weigh self against group, and what helps one may cost the other. Both "put others first" and "put yourself first" are true. (pp. 37–38)
  - Endorphins and dopamine are "selfish": they drive individuals to persevere, find food, build and achieve. Serotonin and oxytocin are "selfless": they drive trust, loyalty, social bonds and cooperation, helping the species survive. (p. 38)
- **Terms:**
  - **"Happy" chemicals:** Sinek's umbrella label for endorphins, dopamine, serotonin and oxytocin, the sources of all positive feelings. (p. 37)
  - **Selfish chemicals:** Endorphins and dopamine, which push individuals to get things done. (p. 38)
  - **Selfless chemicals:** Serotonin and oxytocin, which encourage trust, loyalty and cooperation. (p. 38)
- **Example:** The college dorm, with open doors and social hallways, is the closest modern model of ancestral kinship groups and is where the closest friendships form. (p. 36)
- **Quote:** "We are at our best when we face danger together." (p. 34)

### Chapter 6: E.D.S.O. (pp. 39–48)
- **In one sentence:** Each of the four "happy" chemicals rewards a specific survival behaviour. The selfish ones drive progress but can addict us, while the selfless ones build the trust behind the Circle of Safety.
- **Ideas:**
  - The body rewards behaviours in our interest *before* need becomes urgent (for example, foraging when not hungry). Endorphins and dopamine are the "chemicals of progress," making us feel good when we find, build or achieve. (p. 39)
  - **Endorphins:**
    - Their only purpose is to mask physical pain with pleasure. They are often released in response to stress or fear and cause the "runner's high." (pp. 39–40)
    - They gave our ancestors endurance to track prey over long distances and get home. We can crave them. (p. 40)
    - Today they come mostly from exercise, manual labour and laughter. (p. 40)
  - Laughter releases endorphins, and you cannot laugh and be afraid at the same time. Lightheartedness in tense times relaxes people and lets them focus on the work. (pp. 40–41)
  - **Dopamine:**
    - It produces satisfaction when we find what we seek or complete a task, goal or milestone, such as crossing items off a to-do list. (p. 41)
    - It makes us goal-oriented with a bias for progress. A visible or clearly imagined goal gives a burst to start, each sign of progress gives another, and reaching the goal gives a big hit. (pp. 41–42)
    - Bigger, harder goals yield more dopamine. Hard work toward something difficult feels good, and there is no biological incentive to do nothing. (p. 42)
  - Goals must be tangible. We trust what we can see, so goals we can see or clearly imagine (for example, written down) are more likely to be achieved. (p. 42)
  - Specific, measurable targets motivate; vague ones ("more") don't. Saving is a goal to achieve, not a state of mind. (p. 42)
  - A vision statement must be something we can picture and measure progress toward:
    - "Biggest," "best" or "most respected" are biologically useless.
    - A good vision describes specifically what the world would look like if everything succeeded.
    - The best visions are practically unreachable but worth dying trying for, so every step feels like progress toward something bigger than ourselves. (pp. 42–43)
  - Dopamine is highly addictive. Cocaine, nicotine, alcohol and gambling are all dopamine addictions; only the reinforced behaviour differs. (p. 43)
  - Social media, texts, e-mail and phone notifications hijack dopamine. Checking your phone first thing in the morning is a sign of addiction, and we are less aware of this one than of alcohol or gambling. (pp. 43–44)
  - Performance-driven organizations that reward mainly through dopamine (hit the goal, get the money) can make people addicted to "making the numbers." (p. 44)
  - Shopping, collecting and hobbies satisfy ancient foraging drives through dopamine. Addiction costs time, money and relationships. (p. 44)
  - Dopamine fuels accomplishment, but lasting fulfilment, happiness and loyalty require engagement with others. We remember the friends we made more than the goals we hit. (p. 44)
  - Endorphins and dopamine secure food and shelter, but big things are hard to do alone. Together is better. (pp. 44–45)
  - The *manner* of progress is core to thriving. The selfless chemicals make us feel valued and belonging, inspire work for the group and keep the Circle of Safety strong. (p. 45)
  - Unlike reptiles, which have no reward for cooperation, humans have a mammalian brain layer that made us social. Physically weak alone, we need each other. Serotonin and oxytocin are the backbone of the Circle of Safety. (pp. 45–46)
  - Serotonin and oxytocin build trust and friendship, and are why societies and cultures exist. (p. 46)
  - When these chemicals fire for the right reasons:
    - we feel security, belonging and camaraderie
    - stress falls and fulfilment rises
    - our desire to serve and our trust rise
    - When inhibited, selfishness, aggression, stress, paranoia and mistrust rise, and leadership and cooperation decline. (p. 46)
  - Environments that block these rewards start a vicious cycle: the less others look out for us, the less we look out for them, until everyone loses. (p. 46)
  - These chemicals "grease the social machine." A culture that inhibits them sabotages careers, happiness and the organization. (p. 46)
  - The strength of a culture, not its size or resources, decides an organization's ability to adapt, overcome adversity and innovate. (p. 46)
  - **Serotonin:**
    - It is the feeling of pride when we perceive others like or respect us. It boosts confidence and raises status. (p. 47)
    - As social animals we *need* the approval of our group, which is why awards, recognition programs, ceremonies and "like" counters exist. Recognition must come from others; we cannot get it alone. (p. 47)
    - It reinforces bonds between parent and child, coach and player, and leader and follower. Both the recognized and those who supported them feel pride. (p. 47)
    - Those receiving support feel accountable to their supporters and want to make them proud. Supporters feel responsible for doing right by them. We can feel accountable only to people, never to numbers. (pp. 47–48)
    - The same achievement feels different when others witness and cheer it. (p. 48)
    - Giving to help others succeed earns respect and status, which motivates more giving. It encourages leaders to serve those they are responsible for, and followers to work hard to make leaders proud. (pp. 48–49)
- **Terms:**
  - **E.D.S.O.:** Shorthand for the four "happy" chemicals: Endorphins, Dopamine, Serotonin, Oxytocin. (p. 39)
  - **Chemicals of progress:** Endorphins and dopamine, which reward finding, building and achieving. (p. 39)
  - **Runner's high:** The euphoria from endorphins released during or after hard exertion. (p. 40)
  - **Serotonin, "the leadership chemical":** The chemical of pride and status, felt when others like or respect us. (p. 47)
- **Example:** A vision people can picture, such as Martin Luther King Jr.'s dream of black and white children joining hands, is motivating in a way that "most respected company" is not. (p. 43)
- **Quote:** "Oxytocin and serotonin grease the social machine." (p. 46)
