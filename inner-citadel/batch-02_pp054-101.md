## Part 2: Chapter Breakdowns

### Chapter 4: The Philosopher-Slave and the Emperor-Philosopher (continued) (pp. 54–72)
- **In one sentence:** Marcus read every earlier philosopher through the eyes of a Stoic. His real source was Epictetus, whom he quotes, paraphrases and imitates, and from whom he takes the three rules of life. The supposed influence of Aristo does not hold up.
- **Ideas:**
  - Nature has built human freedom into its plan, including unawareness and resistance. Those who refuse to follow Nature still play the part she foresaw for them. (pp. 54–55)
  - People who live as if asleep are out of tune with reason, so everyday events seem foreign to them. Marcus valued the opposite: feeling at home with Nature, so that whatever Nature brings feels familiar and natural. (p. 55)
  - Marcus rejects advice from Democritus to avoid taking on too much for the sake of peace of mind. Stoics traditionally refused this invitation to withdraw from action. (p. 55)
  - Marcus adopts the saying that the world is nothing but change and life is nothing but opinion. He reads it as Epictetus' idea: things do not trouble us, our judgments about them do. See Chapter 3. (p. 55)
  - Marcus reinterprets Democritus' claim that everything except atoms exists "by convention." For Marcus, everything happens by law, the law of universal Nature, and there are not countless first principles but one, reason, or two, reason and matter. (pp. 55–56)
  - We do know reality. What we wrongly add are labels of good and evil that have no basis in it. (p. 56)
  - Pride and vanity distort our value judgments most of all, and pride fools us most when we think we are doing something serious. (pp. 56–57)
  - Marcus had a gift for recognising Stoic teaching in other writers. He quotes Cynics, Plato, Pythagoreans and Epicurus, but always takes from them what a Stoic could accept. (pp. 57–58)
  - The Plato that Marcus quotes sounds like a Stoic:
    - what matters is justice and injustice, good and evil, not life and death
    - stay at the post you have been given
    - spend your life in the worthiest way rather than trying to save it
    - whoever sees the whole of time and being does not fear death (p. 57)
  - Marcus agrees with Theophrastus that wrongs done for pleasure are worse than wrongs done in anger at an injustice suffered. This fits Stoicism: the sage is perfect and all faults are equal for him, but people who are not yet wise can make progress, so their faults can differ in seriousness. (pp. 57–58)
  - Following the Pythagoreans, look up at the stars at dawn to remind yourself of their order and purity. (p. 58)
  - From Epicurus, rewritten in Stoic terms:
    - be content with what you have and do not regret what you cannot have
    - pain cannot be both unbearable and endless
    - keep the virtues of the ancients in mind
    - never step outside philosophy or slip into the chatter of people who do not practise it (p. 58)
  - Stoics who quote Epicurus are not mixing schools. They know the schools are opposed, but all of them are forms of one thing, the philosophical way of life, so they share some points. (p. 58)
  - Stoics and Epicureans share one practice: focus on the present moment. This shows how precious each instant is, and it reduces pain, because we only ever feel pain in the present moment. (p. 59)
  - Above all, the reading of Epictetus explains the Meditations. Epictetus was the most admired philosopher of the second century and remained a model until the end of antiquity. (pp. 59–60)
  - Epictetus was a former slave who studied under Musonius Rufus. Banished from Rome, he taught in Nicopolis, lived in great poverty and wrote nothing. (pp. 60–61)
  - We know his teaching through his student Arrian, a statesman who also lived as a philosopher. Arrian published the Discourses and the Manual, a pocket selection of maxims to keep "at hand," which is the same need Marcus met by writing the Meditations. (pp. 60–61)
  - The ancients praised Rusticus and Arrian because their philosophy did not stop at pen and ink: they also served the common good. (p. 61)
  - Arrian presents the Discourses as rough notes in everyday language. Epictetus cared only about moving his listeners toward what is better, not about fine words. Scholars disagree about how closely Arrian kept to his exact wording, but the main points come from Epictetus' real teaching. (pp. 62–63)
  - The Discourses are not all of Epictetus' teaching. Most of his class was spent explaining texts by the school's founders, Zeno and Chrysippus. The Discourses record only the informal discussion that followed. (pp. 63–64)
  - So it is wrong to conclude that Epictetus ignored physics or that late Stoicism had become thin in theory. The surviving books are partial, cover only a year or two of thirty years' teaching, and at least one book is lost. (p. 64)
  - Marcus knew more of Epictetus than we do. He had either a fuller copy of Arrian or Rusticus' own lecture notes, and several otherwise unknown sayings of Epictetus survive only through him. (pp. 65–66)
  - Marcus quotes Epictetus by name twice:
    - "you are a little soul carrying around a corpse"
    - when you kiss your child, remind yourself the child may die tomorrow (p. 66)
  - More often, Marcus repeats Epictetus without naming him. He turns a principle into a rule to apply to each case, for example: what does not harm the city does not harm its citizen, so apply this rule whenever you feel injured. (pp. 66–67)
  - The end of Book Eleven of the Meditations reads like notes taken while reading Book Three of the Discourses. It includes a lost passage of Epictetus that sets out the three rules of life. (pp. 67–68)
  - The fight for virtue is no small contest. What is at stake is whether we are sane or mad. (p. 68)
  - A dialogue in Book Four of the Meditations is probably a lost piece of Epictetus. When something happens, do not say "how unlucky I am" but "how lucky I am to stay free of grief." Nothing is a misfortune unless it stops you from being just, generous, self-controlled, careful in judging, truthful and free. (pp. 68–69)
  - Marcus absorbed Epictetus' vocabulary, style and ideas so fully that a medieval copyist wrote that he is "openly Epictetizing." (p. 69)
  - Splitting the soul's acts into judgment, desire and impulse to act is unique to Epictetus. Earlier Stoics did not make this split, and Marcus's three rules come directly from it. See Chapter 3. (pp. 69–70)
  - Epictetus' version of the three rules:
    - find the right rule for giving assent to impressions
    - never relax your attention over impulses to act, so that each has a reserve clause, serves the community and is in proportion to the thing's real value
    - give up desire entirely, and ignore what does not depend on us (p. 70)
  - Marcus was not influenced by Aristo, despite their similar wording about being indifferent to indifferent things:
    - All Stoics teach that only moral good has value and that anything outside our control is neither good nor bad. We must not rank such things but accept them as Nature wills.
    - Aristo went further. For him, indifferent things had no differences at all, which risks total indifference to everything. Mainstream Stoics, including Marcus, still recognised duties to family, society and the state. (pp. 71–72)
  - Aristo thought logic and physics were useless. Marcus only gave up excelling at theoretical logic and physics. He still practised a lived logic, the discipline of judgment, and a lived physics, the discipline of desire, applying them to every impression. (p. 72)
- **Terms:**
  - **Manual, or Encheiridion:** "That which one has at hand." Arrian's pocket selection of Epictetus' maxims, made so that the principles of life are always ready for use. (p. 61)
  - **Kathekonta:** Duties, meaning actions appropriate to human nature in family, social and political life. (p. 72)
  - **Indifferent things:** Everything that does not depend on our freedom, such as wealth, poverty, health and sickness, which is neither good nor bad in itself. (p. 71)
  - **Rule, or kanon:** A principle turned into a test to apply to each particular case. (p. 67)
  - **Tuphos:** Vanity, empty pride, "smoke." (p. 56)
- **Example:** Epictetus' morning exercise: when you see a beautiful person, ask whether their beauty depends on your will. It does not, so let it go. Marcus applies the same kind of rule to feeling injured. (p. 67)
- **Quote:** "For it is above all the reading of Epictetus, and the knowledge of his teachings, which explain the Meditations." (p. 59)

### Chapter 5: The Stoicism of Epictetus (pp. 73–100)
- **In one sentence:** Epictetus' Stoicism, which is Marcus's Stoicism, rests on one reason running through the universe, society and our own thinking. His three disciplines of desire, action and assent are physics, ethics and logic put into daily practice, so they contain the whole of Stoicism.
- **Ideas:**
  - Ancient philosophy was not about inventing a new theory. It meant belonging to a school, explaining its founders and living its way of life. Epictetus kept the core Stoic principles unchanged and added his own way of teaching and some precise distinctions. (p. 73)
  - Stoicism joined three traditions:
    - Socrates' ethics: moral good is the only value, and moral life is a matter of judgment and knowledge
    - Heraclitus' physics: a universe always changing, made of fire, ordered by reason
    - the logic of the Megarians, Plato and Aristotle (pp. 73–74)
  - For a good person, nothing is truly evil, because the only evil is moral evil. Death, illness, loss and insults are not evils for them. Seeing this requires a reasoned dialogue, with others or with oneself. (p. 74)
  - One single reason runs through everything. It links ideas in logic, causes in nature and actions in conduct, so a good person must also be a physicist and a logician. (p. 74)
  - Stoicism is a philosophy of being consistent with oneself:
    - Every living thing instinctively seeks to preserve itself.
    - Humans become moral when reason shows that the highest value is not what instinct seeks, but the choice of agreeing with oneself.
    - This personal consistency matches the consistency of universal Reason, which makes the whole world agree with itself. (p. 75)
  - Wisdom in short: always want the same thing and always refuse the same thing. Only what is morally right can be wanted consistently, and those who live inconsistently are unhappy. (p. 75)
  - Eternal return: Nature's will never changes, so the world repeats endlessly, ending in fire and beginning again exactly the same. The sage, like Reason, wants each moment to happen exactly as it does, forever. (pp. 75–76)
  - The sage is an ideal standard that is almost never met. Only the sage's reason perfectly matches universal Reason. Human reason is part of universal Reason but gets clouded, especially by pleasure. (p. 76)
  - Philosophy is not wisdom but training for wisdom:
    - There is no halfway point between sage and non-sage. One cubit or 500 fathoms under the water, you drown either way.
    - Yet among the non-wise there are two kinds: fools, who do not know their state, and philosophers, who know it and work toward wisdom. So progress is possible. (pp. 76–77)
  - Because only the sage has certain knowledge, Stoic practice works with probabilities. It offers reasonable choices for uncertain daily life. What counts is not the result but the intention to do good, for the sake of moral good alone. (p. 77)
  - Plato's school ranked the parts of philosophy, with logic highest, physics lower and ethics lowest. The Stoics removed the ranking. All three concern the same reason, present in nature, in society and in our thinking, and all reality is one connected, necessary whole. (pp. 78–79)
  - For the Stoics, the parts of philosophy are virtues, and like all virtues they imply each other:
    - the sage practises logic by keeping their judgments consistent
    - ethics by keeping their will and actions consistent
    - physics by acting as a part in harmony with the whole (pp. 78–79)
  - Stoic logic can reach true and certain conclusions, because it reflects the necessary chain of causes in the world. (p. 79)
  - In practice the three parts are one act of agreeing with reason. In teaching they must be separated and put in order, and the logical order of the argument often conflicts with the order the student's growth requires. (p. 80)
  - Stoics disagreed about the teaching order. Logic should come before ethics to give it a rational basis, yet in terms of growth physics should come after ethics, as the crowning revelation. Some taught all three mixed together from the start, because one cannot wait to finish one subject before practising philosophy as a whole. (pp. 80–81)
  - Logic, physics and ethics are parts of discourse *about* philosophy, not of philosophy itself. Lived philosophy means actually speaking well, acting well and living as a true part of the cosmos, rather than theorising about them. (pp. 81–82)
  - Epictetus went back to the strictest early Stoicism of Chrysippus, so his teaching can be read as an outline of Marcus's own Stoicism. (p. 82)
  - Our freedom is an island inside the river of events:
    - Up to us: our judgments, our impulses to act, and our desires and aversions.
    - Not up to us: our body, wealth, honours and office, which depend on the wider course of Nature.
    - Freedom, our true self, and moral good and evil all lie only in the guiding principle. (pp. 83–84)
  - Desire and impulse follow from inner discourse. We desire something because we have told ourselves it is good. See Chapter 3. (p. 84)
  - Accept only "adequate" impressions: ones that stop at what is actually perceived and add nothing extra. This is a better rendering than "comprehensive," which suggests grasping all of reality. (p. 84)
  - Treat impressions like questions in a debate and answer them correctly:
    - "Her son died" is not in our control and is not bad.
    - "He was hurt by it" is in his control and is bad.
    - "He bore it bravely" is good.
    - Report only what happened. If you add "something terrible happened," that comes from you. (pp. 84–85)
  - Calling an event a misfortune goes beyond reality by adding a false value judgment. It happens only in a soul that has not absorbed the core belief that happiness lies only in virtue. (pp. 85–86)
  - The three domains of exercise:
    - **Desire:** give up desiring things outside our control. Desire only moral good and avoid only moral evil. Accept everything else as willed by Nature, which Epictetus calls following the gods. It targets the passions.
    - **Action:** act on appropriate duties toward other people, politics, health and family, guided by the intention to serve the human community and bring about justice.
    - **Assent:** examine every impression so that our judgment adds nothing subjective to what is adequate to reality. (pp. 86–88)
  - Epictetus' three acts of the soul are not Plato's three parts of the soul:
    - For Plato, reason rules lower parts the way rulers rule warriors and workers.
    - For Epictetus, all three acts belong to the one rational guiding principle and are on the same level.
    - Passion is reason gone wrong through bad judgment, and reason itself can be good or bad. (pp. 88–89)
  - Each impulse and desire comes from a judgment, so the soul's impulses and desires follow from what it tells itself. (p. 89)
  - The three disciplines are logic, ethics and physics lived out. The discipline of assent is lived logic, the discipline of impulse is lived ethics and the discipline of desire is lived physics. Epictetus calls each one a "topos," the Stoic word for a part of philosophy. (pp. 89–90)
  - Life is made of more than books. Reading treatises on logic or impulses is useless without actually testing real impressions and actually acting. (pp. 90–91)
  - The discipline of desire is lived physics because accepting fate requires knowing our place in the whole:
    - Desire only what is up to you, and accept the rest as willed by universal Reason.
    - Events are woven together by Reason, and you are a tiny part of the whole. (pp. 91–92)
  - Epictetus orders the disciplines differently depending on the purpose:
    - **In daily practice:** criticising impressions comes first, because impressions cause both passions and actions. All three are really one activity, the right use of impressions.
    - **In teaching:** desire comes first, because it frees us from worry and grief. Action comes second, because it teaches us to live in family and city. Technical logic comes last, for advanced students. (pp. 92–94)
  - Physics is taught so that we can tell good from evil, and that distinction is what the discipline of desire rests on. Study how the world is run, then consider who you are and what is good and evil for you. (p. 94)
  - Rational beings can reflect that they are parts of the whole and that it is good for parts to give way to the whole. (p. 95)
  - Lived physics also means enjoying the sight of the universe as God sees it. Like Zeus alone at the end of a world cycle, we should talk with ourselves, reflect on how the world is governed, and examine our attitude to what happens to us. (p. 95)
  - Do not fight God or complain when what was given is taken back. Body, possessions and family all belong to the turning of the universe. Life is a short stay to watch God's government and share his festival, and a good person leaves it grateful. (pp. 95–96)
  - Humans were placed here to contemplate God and his works. Do not die without having contemplated them. (p. 96)
  - Logic belongs at both ends of training:
    - **At the start:** learning to criticise impressions and accept only adequate ones.
    - **At the end:** the theory of syllogisms, which makes the principles of action unshakable.
    - Its danger is that it becomes a technical end in itself or a way to show off. (pp. 96–98)
  - Never accept an unexamined impression, just as Socrates said the unexamined life is not worth living. (p. 97)
  - The three disciplines set our relationship to the whole of reality:
    - Desire puts us back in the cosmos as a part that conforms to the will of the whole.
    - Action puts us back in human society, acting by the reason all people share.
    - Judgment lets us follow the logical necessity of the reason within us. (pp. 98–99)
  - The philosopher must drop a partial, selfish view of things and see as universal Reason sees. A part has no good of its own apart from the common good. (p. 99)
  - The philosopher is a citizen of the world and also of the human city, its smaller image. Caring about the whole universe does not cancel social duties. All people share reason, so all are brothers and sisters, even a slave and their master. (p. 99)
  - Freedom lets us join the cosmos's rational order willingly, but it also lets inconsistency into our thoughts, feelings and society. Our agreement with Reason is always fragile, yet the divine plan will happen regardless. (p. 100)
  - The three disciplines contain the whole of Stoicism and ask for a complete reversal of our usual view of the world and way of living. (p. 100)
- **Terms:**
  - **Consistency with oneself:** Living by one harmonious rule of life. The core principle of Stoicism, grounded in the self-consistency of universal Reason. (p. 75)
  - **Eternal return:** The Stoic belief that the world endlessly repeats, exactly the same, because Nature's will never changes. (pp. 75–76)
  - **Sage:** The ideal wise person whose reason fully matches universal Reason. An almost unreachable standard. (p. 76)
  - **Philosopher:** Someone who is not yet wise, knows it and trains for wisdom. (p. 77)
  - **Adequate impression:** One that stops at what is perceived and adds nothing extra. Hadot's preferred rendering of kataleptike. (p. 84)
  - **Topos, or exercise theme:** Epictetus' name for each of the three disciplines. It is also the Stoic word for a part of philosophy. (p. 90)
  - **Assent, or sunkatathesis:** The act of accepting an impression or judgment as true. (p. 87)
  - **Right use of impressions:** Epictetus' name for the single activity that includes all three disciplines. (p. 93)
- **Example:** "His ship sank." Asked what happened, answer only "his ship sank." Adding "a terrible thing happened to him" is your own false judgment. (p. 85)
- **Quote:** "Thus, logic, physics, and ethics are distinguishable when we talk about philosophy, but not when we live it." (p. 82)

### Chapter 6: The Inner Citadel, or the Discipline of Assent (pp. 101–127)
*Only the first page of this chapter is in this batch.*
- **In one sentence:** Marcus wrote the Meditations to build, within his life as emperor, the inner discourse needed to practise Epictetus' three rules. The discipline of assent means accepting only objective impressions, which starts with understanding how sensation produces an image in the soul.
- **Ideas:**
  - The Meditations are Stoic spiritual exercises for practising Epictetus' three rules in the middle of an emperor's life. They have the same three-part structure as Epictetus' teaching. See Chapter 3. (p. 101)
  - The discipline of assent means refusing to let in any impression that is not objective, or adequate. (p. 101)
  - Sensation is a bodily process we share with animals. It carries the impression of an outside object to the soul and produces an image of it in the soul's guiding part. (p. 101)
  - The image has two sides. It stands in for the object, and it is also a change in the soul caused by that object. (p. 101)
- **Terms:**
  - **Sensation, or aisthesis:** The bodily process that carries an outside object's impression to the soul. (p. 101)
  - **Pathos:** Here, a change produced in the soul by the action of an outside object. (p. 101)
- **Example:** none needed.
- **Quote:** "As we have seen, the Meditations are Stoic spiritual exercises." (p. 101)
