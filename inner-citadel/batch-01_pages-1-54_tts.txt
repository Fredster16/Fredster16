The Inner Citadel. A reduced version for listening. Batch one, covering pages 1 to 54.

This is Pierre Hadot's study of the Meditations of Marcus Aurelius, translated by Michael Chase. It is a book about the Meditations, not the Meditations itself.


Part one. Book map.

The book's central claim. The Meditations are not a diary and not a treatise. They are spiritual exercises that Marcus wrote for himself. In them he keeps restating the Stoic principles of Epictetus, and three disciplines of life, so that they would shape how he lived.

The chapters. Chapters four to ten, and the conclusion, are summarised here from their section headings only. Full breakdowns will follow in later batches.

Chapter one. The Emperor-Philosopher. Marcus became a philosopher in the ancient sense, by choosing a way of life. His conversion was gradual, and was led mainly by Junius Rusticus, who introduced him to Epictetus.

Chapter two. A First Glimpse of the Meditations. The Meditations survived by chance, have no original title, and have been misread as a treatise or a diary. They are personal notes, which the ancients called hupomnemata, that Marcus wrote for himself.

Chapter three. The Meditations as Spiritual Exercises. The work keeps restating Stoic principles and three rules of life: judgment, desire, and action. Marcus uses writing and imagination to keep them alive in his mind.

Chapter four. The Philosopher-Slave and the Emperor-Philosopher. Marcus's sources include quotations from other philosophers, but above all Epictetus, from whom his three disciplines come. The chapter also weighs the influence of the Stoic Aristo.

Chapter five. The Stoicism of Epictetus. The main features of Stoicism: being consistent with oneself, the parts of philosophy, three acts of the soul matched to three themes of exercise, and consent to destiny.

Chapter six. The Inner Citadel, or the Discipline of Assent. We should accept only objective impressions. Things cannot touch the soul, our judgment is free, and we should draw a boundary around the true self.

Chapter seven. The Discipline of Desire, or Love of Fate. Focus on the present moment. Become aware of the whole cosmos. Choose providence over chance. Describe things in plain physical terms. Take the view from above, and love your fate.

Chapter eight. The Discipline of Action, or Action in the Service of Mankind. Act seriously and with pure intention, while accepting in advance that things may turn out otherwise. Practise altruism, justice, fairness, gentleness, and love of neighbour.

Chapter nine. Virtue and Joy. Each discipline matches a virtue. Truth matches the discipline of assent. Self-control matches the discipline of desire. Justice matches the discipline of action. Joy comes from living in line with Nature.

Chapter ten. Marcus Aurelius in His Meditations. There are limits to reading Marcus's personality from the text. The chapter covers his style, the dating of each book of the Meditations, the first book as his confessions, his solitude, and his political role models.

The conclusion asks what lasting value the attitudes in the Meditations still have.

How the main ideas connect.

First. Philosophy as a way of life, from chapter one, explains why Marcus wrote at all. He was training himself, not adding to doctrine.

Second. The genre of personal notes, from chapter two, explains the work's form: private, unordered, and repetitive. That form only makes sense as spiritual exercise, which is the subject of chapter three.

Third. Core principles support the three rules of life. Freedom of judgment supports the discipline of assent. Providence and a rational universe support the discipline of desire. The community of rational beings supports the discipline of action. Chapters six, seven, and eight cover each discipline in turn.

Fourth. Epictetus is the source of both the principles and the three disciplines. The Meditations are variations on his themes.

Fifth. The disciplines lead to virtue and joy, and this framework lets us read Marcus's personality from the text without over-reading it.


Part two. Chapter breakdowns.


Chapter one. The Emperor-Philosopher.

In one sentence. Marcus became a philosopher in the ancient sense, by adopting a way of life rather than writing theory, through a gradual conversion guided mainly by Junius Rusticus and the teachings of Epictetus.

The ideas.

Marcus's reign was a constant struggle against war, rebellion, plague, natural disasters, and family grief. He must be understood against this background.

The book's aim is not biography. It asks how Marcus became a philosopher, and how the Meditations formed part of his philosophical activity.

In the ancient world, a philosopher was someone who lived a philosophical life, not necessarily someone who wrote theory. A person could be a philosopher without ever writing a treatise.

For the great ancient teachers, real philosophy was how one lived, not talking about theory or commenting on authors.

Ancient philosophers did not need to write. If they did write, they did not need to invent a new theory. Restating their school's principles was enough. Marcus added nothing new to Stoic doctrine, yet he was a true Stoic philosopher.

Taking philosophy classes did not make someone a philosopher. Many speakers and statesmen used philosophy only as training in argument, and as material for their speeches.

To be a philosopher meant declaring, after a conversion, a way of life different from that of other people.

Marcus's path to philosophy had stages. First, a carefree youth until about the age of twenty. Second, an early attraction in childhood, through his teacher Diognetus, to the simple, austere life of the philosopher. Third, his main conversion through Rusticus, around the years 144 to 147, with the first evidence of it at about the age of twenty-five.

The rough cloak and the hard bed were symbols of the Stoic and Cynic life. They drew on an idealised picture of Sparta. From it, the philosophers kept only the training in endurance, the return to a natural life, and contempt for social conventions.

A statesman could also be a philosophy teacher. Philosophy was not limited to specialists, and young Romans were traditionally guided by older mentors in both political and moral life.

What Marcus learned from Rusticus. Reform your own character. Avoid clever arguments for their own sake, learned treatises, showy speeches, and displays of virtue. Write simply, and read texts carefully. Be quick to make peace with people who have offended you. Above all, Rusticus gave him access to notes from the lectures of Epictetus.

Following Epictetus, the goal of philosophy is to reason correctly and to correct one's character, not outward austerity, clever argument, or display. True philosophy lies in simplicity.

The first book of the Meditations lists the role models and practical advice Marcus received. It is not a history of his ideas. Epictetus was the greatest gift that Rusticus gave him, and the Meditations are variations on Epictetus' themes.

Marcus's conversion was not a sudden event. It was a slow change through teachers and reading. A letter about his reading of a writer named Aristo shows how deeply philosophy shook him, but his key influence was Epictetus.

Hadot argues against reading that letter as being about law books. In it, Marcus says his soul has not yet taken in the healing principles of philosophy. That fits philosophy, not law.

From his teachers Apollonius and Sextus, Marcus took moral attitudes and practical advice, not doctrine. Balance opposites: decide after reflection, but without delay; be alert, yet relaxed. Be kind, and live according to nature. Combine calm with tenderness. And learning is good at any age.

Marcus studied all three parts of philosophy: ethics, physics, and logic. The Stoics of his day mostly followed Musonius Rufus and his student Epictetus.

The first book of the Meditations has a clear order. It moves from his parents, to his early teachers, to Rusticus and his conversion, to his school professors, his teachers of grammar and speaking, his philosopher friends, the emperor Antoninus as the ideal ruler, and finally the gods.

Marcus was openly a Stoic. His speaking teacher Fronto worried that philosophy might lead him to bad decisions, and might undermine the eloquence a ruler needs. Fronto stood for common sense against philosophical strictness.

Marcus governed with statesmen who had committed themselves to rival schools, Stoic and Aristotelian. In effect, several philosophers ruled the Empire.

The emperor's love of philosophy also led some people to pretend to be philosophers, in the hope of winning his favour.

Key terms.

Conversion to philosophy. A radical change of lifestyle, by which someone adopts a philosophical way of life different from that of other people.

The Spartan mirage. The philosophers' idealised picture of Spartan austerity, kept only for its training in endurance and its natural simplicity.

An example. Cato the Younger never wrote a philosophical treatise, yet he counted as a Stoic philosopher because he lived as one.

A quote from the chapter. In antiquity, a philosopher was someone who lived like a philosopher, that is, who led a philosophical life.


Chapter two. A First Glimpse of the Meditations.

In one sentence. The Meditations survived by chance and had no title. Readers have long misjudged what kind of work they are. They are personal, day-to-day notes that Marcus wrote for himself, in carefully polished language, as spiritual exercises.

The ideas.

Any ancient text that survived until the age of printing was almost a miracle. The survival of the Meditations was especially fragile. There is no sign that they circulated widely. They were rediscovered by Byzantine readers in the tenth century, first printed in 1559, and today only one complete manuscript remains.

Some passages are damaged and can only be restored by guesswork. Hadot's reading rests on his own translation, which is why he quotes Marcus so often.

The usual chapter divisions date from an edition of 1652 and need revising. Some chapters should be joined, and many should be split.

In the ancient world, titles usually came from students or from the subject matter, not from the author. Unpublished notes normally had no title. Marcus almost certainly gave his private notes none.

The many titles given to the work later reflect different theories about what it is. The title chosen by the scholar Meric Casaubon, About Himself and To Himself, captures it best. The work is a dialogue with oneself about the rule of one's own life, where the self means the soul or the mind.

Later readers misread the work by projecting the habits of their own time onto it. Some saw it as a damaged or jumbled systematic treatise. Some saw it as a draft handbook of advice. And some saw it as a confessional diary.

The scholar Thomas Gataker came closest. He saw the work as rough working notes, ordered only by when and where they were written. That explains why they repeat themselves and are sometimes obscure.

It is a spiritual diary only in the sense of notes written day by day. It is not an outpouring of feelings, so it cannot tell us whether Marcus had an anxious soul.

The work mixes several forms. There are short, striking sayings. There are longer passages: dialogues, encouragements to himself, and discussions of general questions. There are rare passages about his own role and situation. There are exercises of the imagination that prepare for death. And there are two collections of quotations, chosen for their moral effect.

Notes of place, such as written in the land of the Quadi, were probably written by Marcus himself. If so, they are unique in ancient literature, and they show that the writing followed his changing inner state.

In the second and third books, facing death on campaign, Marcus changed the purpose of his writing. Instead of collecting extracts out of curiosity, he wrote only thoughts that would transform how he lived.

With death close, one thing matters: keeping the essential rules of life always in mind. These are: control your inner speech, act only for the good of the human community, and accept whatever the course of Nature brings.

The Meditations belong to an ancient genre of personal notes, kept day by day. This was a common practice among educated people. They used such notes both to inform themselves and to shape themselves, that is, to make spiritual progress.

Unlike other collections of notes, Marcus's notes are mostly encouragements to himself and inner dialogue, written with great care.

Writing by hand, rather than dictating to a secretary, suits private reflection. Another person's presence makes our inner speech ordinary and impersonal. So Marcus wrote the Meditations in his own hand.

Ancient works passed through three stages: rough drafts, a provisional version, and a final revision for publication. Marcus stayed at the first stage, probably writing on wax tablets. Each book has its own vocabulary and themes, which suggests each was written while he was focused on one question.

Three things are certain. Marcus wrote for himself. He wrote day by day, not as a single work for the public. And he wrote in a refined literary style, because the perfection of the wording gave the words their psychological power and persuasive force.

What sets Marcus's notes apart is that they were spiritual exercises, following a specific method.

Key terms.

Hupomnemata. Personal notes taken day by day, used to aid memory and to help one's own growth, not originally meant for publication.

Adversaria. Gataker's word for rough working notes kept close at hand.

An example. A woman named Pamphila published thirteen years of her notes in no particular order, and deliberately kept the disorder. This is the same genre as Marcus's notes.

A quote from the chapter. Thus, the Meditations belong to that type of writing called hupomnemata in antiquity.


Chapter three. The Meditations as Spiritual Exercises.

In one sentence. Behind their apparent disorder, the Meditations are a tightly organised system. Marcus repeats Stoic principles, and three disciplines of life covering judgment, desire, and action, in writing and in imagination, to keep them alive in himself.

The ideas.

The work's only theme is philosophy, understood as a way of life. It keeps sketching the ideal good person. This person acts justly in the service of others, calmly accepts what is outside their control, and thinks truthfully. Ordinary people, by contrast, think carelessly, act at random, and resent what happens to them.

The three practical rules express an inner choice and a view of the world. That view is put into words as core principles: universal principles that ground and justify particular actions.

The basic Stoic principle is this. The only good is moral good, which is virtue, and the only evil is moral evil, which is vice. Happiness means doing what human nature truly requires, guided by principles that tell good from bad.

Living well is an art, and like every art it needs principles. Some concern the nature of the whole universe, and some concern human nature.

These principles fade unless they are constantly rekindled, through the inner images that make them present to us.

Apart from the first book, the whole work is a constant restating of the three rules of action and the principles behind them.

Marcus learned the proofs of these principles in the Stoic schools and from Epictetus. A short statement is enough to recall a proof, and to renew the will to do good.

Restating the principles is a retreat into oneself, rather than into the countryside. The phrases should be brief and essential, so that they work fully.

The first method is short lists of key points. They act as memory aids, and piling the points up makes them stronger. Gathered together, these lists cover almost every theme of the work.

From the idea that only moral good is good, several things follow. Pleasure and pain are not evils. Wrongs done to us cannot truly touch us. A wrongdoer harms only himself. And fault lies only within oneself.

Only what depends on us can be good or evil, and our judgment depends on us. So all our troubles lie in our own judgments. People cause their own problems. Everything is a matter of judgment. And every fault is a false judgment, born of ignorance.

The Stoics chose providence over the idea that the world is just atoms moving by chance. From the unity and rationality of the world, several things follow. Everything, even human malice, comes from universal Nature. All things change constantly, and yet repeat. We must die. In everything, we must separate the cause, which is reason, from the material. And shared reason, not shared blood, makes people kin, so people are made for one another.

Against the vastness of Nature, of space, and of time, life is very short, the present moment is tiny, and the earth is a speck. Fame is empty, especially when it comes from people who contradict themselves and do not deserve respect.

Each discipline rests on its own group of principles. The discipline of thought rests on freedom of judgment. The discipline of action rests on the community of rational beings. And the discipline of consent rests on providence.

The second method is repeating single principles on their own. For example, the idea that our troubles come only from our judgments appears eighteen times. The repetition is deliberate.

All things have the same content, and repeat endlessly. Whoever has seen the present has seen everything, so the length of a life does not matter.

It is not enough to repeat the principles. Their theoretical basis, which Marcus calls the science of Nature, must stay present and active in the mind. Otherwise the phrases lose their meaning.

The third method is rebuilding the arguments and working through the difficulties. For example: a city is a group of beings under one law. The world is a group of beings under one law, the law of Reason. So the world is a city. These arguments appear either as polished set pieces or as technical discussions.

The three rules match three activities of the soul and three areas of reality. Judgment concerns our own power of judgment, and requires objectivity. Desire concerns universal Nature, and requires consent to destiny. The impulse to act concerns human nature, and requires justice and care for others.

This three-part model appears often. Sometimes all three appear together, sometimes in pairs, sometimes one at a time, and sometimes in a very compressed shorthand. Marcus also lists groups of three virtues that match the disciplines: truth, justice, and self-control.

The three disciplines are the true key to the Meditations. Each group of principles forms around one of them. So beneath the apparent disorder lies a rigorous system of ideas.

Exercises of the imagination. Marcus does not only reason; he also pictures things. He imagines whole royal courts and whole generations of the past, and famous people now long dead. This lets him see for himself the principle that everything changes and passes away.

A life not lit by virtue, by the rules of life, and by knowledge of the principles, is a kind of death. That is why Marcus gives harsh, Cynic-style portraits of how pointless human affairs can be.

The many repetitions, some almost word for word, are variations on a few themes.

Phrases such as separate the cause from the material have a personal meaning for Marcus. They remind him of the guiding part of the mind within him. This guiding part makes us independent of the body, and marks out what is in our power.

These phrases never set out a doctrine. They act as triggers. By association, they reawaken ideas and practices Marcus already knows well. That is why he never needs to give examples.

Marcus writes to keep the principles always at hand. He is following Epictetus' advice to meditate on them, write them down, and read them every day.

The Stoic life means mastering one's inner speech. Everything depends on how we picture things to ourselves. Things themselves do not trouble us; our judgments about them do.

There are two kinds of therapy. The first is the teacher's spoken word, which reshapes the listeners' inner speech through striking phrases, reasoning, and imagery. The second is writing for oneself, which takes in those principles until they become the principles of one's own inner speech.

The principles are not rules learned once and applied mechanically. They must become intense, personal realisations and moral experiences. And these fade quickly.

Rereading does not bring them back, because written pages are already dead. What matters is saying them again in the moment, as carefully as possible, for the greatest effect. This is why the work is so repetitive.

The goal is to keep reawakening an inner state that is always in danger of going numb, and to bring order back to an inner speech scattered by routine. The task never ends, because the true philosopher knows he is not yet wise.

Marcus wrote in Greek for several reasons. Greek was the language of philosophy, even in Rome. Stoic technical terms lose meaning in translation. He was writing for himself, not trying to make philosophy popular. And with death near, he needed Epictetus' exact wording straight away, rather than spending time translating it.

Philosophy, like poetry, cannot be fully translated, so technical terms must be translated with great care.

Key terms.

Dogma, or core principle. A universal principle that grounds and justifies a particular way of acting, and that can be stated in one or more sentences.

Theorem. A principle of the art of living. Marcus uses it to mean the same as a core principle.

Kephalaia. Short lists of key points that sum up the core principles, used as memory aids.

Phantasia. A representation: the inner image that makes a principle present to the mind. It does not mean imagination.

Hegemonikon. The guiding part of the mind: the power of thought and judgment that makes us independent of the body, and marks out what is in our power.

Of identical content. The idea that all things repeat endlessly.

Hupolepsis. A value judgment.

Kataleptikos. Objective, or adequate.

Epakolouthesis. A necessary but non-essential consequence.

Hupexairesis. A reserve clause: accepting in advance that things may turn out otherwise.

The three disciplines, or rules of life. The discipline of judgment, also called assent. The discipline of desire, which means consent to destiny. And the discipline of action, which means impulses directed toward justice.

An example. In one passage, Marcus lists eight points to remember whenever he is annoyed. Among them: everything happens according to Nature, and everything is a matter of judgment. These are tools for calmly accepting events.

A quote from the chapter. These three disciplines of life are the true key to the Meditations of Marcus Aurelius.


Chapter four. The Philosopher-Slave and the Emperor-Philosopher.

Only the first page of this chapter is included in this batch.

In one sentence. Marcus's occasional quotations from earlier philosophers, especially Heraclitus, may come from his Stoic reading rather than from the originals. Heraclitus' idea that most people live as if asleep led Marcus to conclude that everyone, even the unaware and the resistant, contributes to the world's single work.

The ideas.

The quotations in the Meditations may come from Marcus's own reading, or second-hand through Stoic writers.

The Stoics treated Heraclitus as their ancestor. His words appear in the Meditations, but it is hard to tell quotation from paraphrase, perhaps because Marcus was quoting from memory.

Following Heraclitus, most people live without awareness, as if asleep, speaking and acting without knowing what they do.

Everyone works toward one single work, some knowingly and some without knowing it. Even those who resist universal Reason cooperate with it, because the course of Nature also needs those who refuse to follow it.

A quote from Marcus, given in the chapter. We are all working together in order to complete one work.
